For Firms

Six Moves That Make a First Year

On your site: behind your member login

12%

of employees strongly agree their organization does a great job onboarding new hires

Gallup workplace research, across companies of every size

The bar is that low. A firm with no training department can clear it with a page of paper and a calendar. At most small and mid-size firms, new hires belong to whoever hired them, with no program behind them; the six moves that matter do not require one. We graded the evidence for each; less is rigorously proven about onboarding than vendors claim.

1

Write the expectations down.

Role clarity is the best-documented move on this menu, and it costs an hour. Give every new hire a one-page sheet on day one: the behaviors you want to see, who they go to with questions, and what on track looks like at 30, 60, and 90 days. New hires say it over and over: nobody told me what was expected. A page they can reread outlasts anything said in orientation week.

What goes on the page: the five or six behaviors you actually care about (the first-90-days checklist on this hub is a ready starting list), the two or three people a new hire can always ask, and one sentence each on what good looks like at 30, 60, and 90 days. Write it once for your next new hire; reuse it for every hire after. [SOCIETY NAME] can send you a starting outline to mark up.

2

Give the peer mentor a calendar.

A named person and a standing time on two calendars; frequency drives the effect. Name one person, block time on both calendars, and meet at least weekly for the first quarter. "Come find me anytime" is a greeting, not a plan.

In one company’s 600-person pilot:

56%

of new hires who met their onboarding buddy once in the first 90 days said the buddy helped them become productive quickly

97%

among those who met more than eight times

Microsoft onboarding pilot, via Harvard Business Review, 2019. Frequency is the mechanism.

3

Put them in front of clients early.

Week three is the cheapest time to find out whether the phone will be a problem. Bring new hires into live calls in the first few weeks, give them one small speaking part, then debrief: what did they notice, what would they ask next time. You learn in week three, while the stakes are small, whether the phone will be a problem.

4

Move internships ahead of busy season where you can.

The internship is the honest preview, and busy season is when it tells the truth. An intern who works a real busy season does real staff work; a summer intern too often gets whatever the office could scrape together that week. Busy season is the honest preview, for both sides, before an offer is on the table.

68.7%

of hires who interned with their employer are still there a year later

40.3%

for hires with no internship experience

NACE internship and co-op research, 2020. The gap persists at five years.

5

Close the loop with the schools and career centers you hire from.

One email a year per school buys a better-prepared class. When an intern or new hire struggled, tell the program what was missing: patterns, never a named person. One email a year, after busy season: the two or three gaps you saw most, and one thing their students did well. It is free curriculum feedback for them and a better-prepared class for you, and it makes yours the firm the department thinks of when strong students ask where to apply. If you do not have a contact, [SOCIETY NAME] can make the introduction.

6

Schedule the feedback.

Fifteen scheduled minutes a week beats a saved-up review. Short, regular check-ins on the calendar from week one, not saved for review season. Fifteen minutes weekly covers it: what went well, one thing to change next week, and whatever questions they have been sitting on. New hires who hear nothing assume the worst and stall; the ones who hear something weekly correct course while correcting is still cheap.

54%

of managers say they give their early-career staff feedback on communication

20%

of that staff say they receive it

The Illinois CPA Society’s 2025 readiness study. Whatever you believe you already deliver, schedule it anyway.

What firms are actually doing

Practices firm leaders described to us in spring 2026, already running at real firms, each one a live version of a move above:

  • Two weeks of structured onboarding on the firm's software stack before client work starts (move 1, scaled up).
  • Interns started in spring busy season rather than summer, deliberately (move 4).
  • Outreach to students 12 to 18 months before internship eligibility: tours, panels, networking (move 4, upstream).
  • College classes brought into the firm for a working day, not just the career fair (move 5).
  • Recruiting by teaching: presenting at schools on the topics students ask about, employer second (move 5, upstream).
  • New-hire and client-meeting simulations that rebuild the practice reps technology has removed from live work (move 3, before the stakes are real).

What to stop doing

  • Assuming they will pick it upNobody reliably picks up what was never said out loud. Your partners learned by watching; today's new hires get less time in the room to watch, so the unwritten rules have to become written ones.
  • Manufacturing busywork for internsThey can tell, and it teaches the wrong lesson: that the firm has nothing real for them. Give interns small pieces of real work (move 4), or hire a smaller class.
  • Saving feedback for annual reviewsBy then the habits have set, and so has their impression of the firm. The correction that takes one sentence in March is a hard conversation in November.

The one-hour version

Write the one-page expectations sheet for your next new hire, or your current one; late beats never. It takes an hour, and it is the highest-graded move on the menu.
