I run the research operation at the Pennsylvania Institute of CPAs and its national research arm, CPA Crossings. The papers I write there go out under the names of state CPA societies across the country.

Before this I spent a decade in cognitive science, studying how people learn, attend, and decide. That turned out to be the right preparation for the question most organizations are now working through: what happens when skilled people are handed AI, and what it takes for them to keep using it.

The research operation

PICPA is the second-oldest state CPA society in the country, with about 20,000 members. I took its research function from one publication line to the evidence base behind membership, workforce, advocacy, and revenue decisions. Some of the pieces:

  • The Pennsylvania CPA Census

    A full-population portrait of the state's profession from the public licensing roster, reported in aggregate with small-cell suppression. The first whole-population evidence base the organization has had.

  • Membership to 2035

    A cohort-replacement forecast, backtested to under 1% error, with a scenario simulator leadership uses to test recruitment, retention, and pricing levers.

  • The Missing 62%

    Roughly 16,000 licensed CPAs in the state were not members. A four-stage AI pipeline researched them from public sources, passed an accuracy audit with zero wrong entries, and surfaced a seven-figure opportunity. Built alone, on a flat $200-a-month subscription.

  • Peer-society intelligence

    A verified base covering 17 state CPA societies: offerings, dues mechanics, financials. Built with paired gather-and-verify agents and adversarial checking, so every claim traces to a source.

  • The member survey

    The organization's largest member survey to date, analyzed end to end: the outcome of a board-level KPI, the strongest driver of perceived value, and a weighting caveat on the reported NPS.
