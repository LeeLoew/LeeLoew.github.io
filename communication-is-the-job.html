Communication Is the Job

On your site: behind the free member login

“The differentiator will be who can talk to clients, not just who can do the work.”

A small-firm leader at a roundtable of CPAs, spring 2026

The technical work matters, and firms will train you on it. Your end of that deal is picking the training up quickly: absorb it, use it on live work, and adapt when the next engagement looks nothing like the example. What firms say they cannot find is a new hire who is comfortable talking with clients. That part is learnable, and the fastest way to learn it is to treat communication as part of the job itself, practiced on purpose, starting before the first message goes out.

Before you reach out

Most first-year communication fear is reasonable. You are about to ask a client for something, and you are not sure you should. Two mistakes make the fear come true: asking for something the client already sent, and asking for something you cannot explain when the client says "why do you need that?" Both are avoidable, and each check is a question inside your own firm before it is a question to a client:

  • Has it already been providedCheck the portal, the shared folder, and the prior-year file, then ask your team. Clients notice when they are asked twice.
  • Do you know why you are askingIf the client asks what it is for, you need one plain sentence. If you do not have one yet, ask your senior first; the answer usually takes thirty seconds.
  • Has someone else already reached outOne point of contact per request. Check with whoever last talked to the client.

Then reach out. The fear does not go away by waiting; it goes away by preparing and then doing the prepared thing. Four drills cover most of it.

1

The call

After two messages without resolution, call. If an email or chat thread has gone back and forth twice and the question is still open, a third message usually just adds to the pile. A five-minute call ends it.

MessageMessageCall

“The biggest gap is inability to communicate... they won't get on the phone.”

The same firm leader, in the same conversation

The first calls feel awkward, so use a script:

Opening line

Hi, this is [your name]. I'm working on your [return / project / file] and have one quick question. Is now an okay time?

The ask

Before I go further, I want to make sure I have this right. Can you walk me through [the specific item]?

The close

That's exactly what I needed. I'll send a short note confirming what we covered, and you'll have [the next piece] by [day].

Name the fear honestly: calls feel high-stakes because you cannot edit yourself in real time the way you can in a message. Everyone new feels that, and it stops feeling that way with reps. Nobody remembers your phone voice. They remember that you called. Rehearse it first: prompt 1 below plays the client for you.

2

The client conversation

Three habits handle most client contact:

Before

Prepare one sentence on where things stand.

Before any client contact, be ready to say what is done, what is open, and what you need from them. If you cannot say it in one sentence, you are not ready for the conversation yet.

During

Ask before assuming.

When something in the file does not make sense, the client can usually explain it in thirty seconds. Guessing feels faster and almost never is.

After

Write down what you agreed to before you hang up.

Decisions, deadlines, who owes what. Then send a two-line recap so both sides are working from the same record.

And notice what that widest gap actually is: not vocabulary, calibration. The way you explain a reconciliation to a client is not the way you flag it to your senior. Before each conversation, ask yourself who this is for and what they need to walk away knowing. Prompt 2 below makes you explain it before a real client asks.

3

The email

The pattern: a subject line that says the ask, three sentences, one question.

Short, specific, easy to answer. The greeting gets its own line and a comma, then a blank line: it is an email, not a text. If your email needs more than three sentences, it probably needs to be a call instead. Prompt 3 below stress-tests your draft before you send it.

4

The meeting

The people who seem sharp in meetings did their homework before walking in. Nobody assigns it, and everyone notices it:

Scan the materials before you walk in. All of them, even the attachments nobody mentions.

Know the client background: who they are, what the engagement is, and what happened the last time anyone talked to them.

Speak once early. Ask one prepared question or offer one observation in the first stretch of the meeting. The longer you wait, the harder it gets, and people form their read on you either way.

In the roomDress a notch upPhone awayNotes people can seeWhy you are in the room

Much of this happens in person, in a conference room rather than on a screen, and the read people form there includes how you carry yourself. Dress a notch above what the room expects until you know the room, keep the phone put away, take notes people can see, and when you introduce yourself, say why you are in the room: "I'm Sam, and I'm covering the fixed assets on this engagement." Nobody at the table needs your years of service; they need to know what to bring you. If the room trades tenure, add yours after. All of it says the same thing: you came ready.

Rehearse with AI before you dial

Here is a practice trick nobody teaches: run the conversation against an AI tool first. Paste in your draft email or your opening line and ask what a client would misread, where you hedged, and what you forgot to ask. Five minutes of rehearsal takes the edge off the real one.

Before you paste anything into an AI tool: know your firm's AI policy first, and keep client names, numbers, and documents out of any tool the firm has not approved. Use made-up details when you practice. The prompts below are invented on purpose.

Three prompts to try, pasted straight in, one per drill:

Paste"You are a small-business client whose March bank statement is late. I am the first-year calling to ask for it. Play the client, push back a little, and afterward tell me where I hedged and what I forgot to ask."
Paste"I am about to explain to a client why we need their fixed-asset detail. Ask me the three questions a confused client would actually ask."
Paste"Here is my draft email. Tell me what a busy client would misread, what they might skip, and what I buried that should have been the first line."

Voice practice counts double; saying it out loud to a voice-capable AI tool is closer to the real call than typing ever gets. Verify anything the tool tells you about the accounting itself; that habit is AI With Judgment. And ask [SOCIETY NAME] what practice tools it offers members.
